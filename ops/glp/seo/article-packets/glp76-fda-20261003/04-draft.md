# Compounded Tirzepatide vs Zepbound: 2026 FDA Status Guide

> **Quick answer:** Compounded tirzepatide and Zepbound are not the same regulatory product. FDA identifies Zepbound as a tirzepatide injection and lists it as prescription. FDA says compounded drugs are not FDA-approved and are not reviewed before marketing for safety, effectiveness, or quality. A shared ingredient does not prove equivalence. [S1][S2][S4][S6]

## The short FDA answer

The current FDA-hosted label identifies Zepbound as a tirzepatide injection for subcutaneous use. [S4] The Drugs@FDA application record lists ZEPBOUND, active ingredient TIRZEPATIDE, a solution for subcutaneous administration, and prescription marketing status. [S6]

FDA’s compounding Q&A states that compounded drugs are not FDA-approved. [S2] FDA’s unapproved-GLP-1 statement says the agency does not review compounded drugs for safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed. [S1]

Those statements are not contradictory. The first set identifies one approved product and its label. The second describes a category of compounded drugs. The shared ingredient name does not transfer the approved product’s label, presentation, manufacturing controls, clinical evidence, or regulatory status to an unnamed preparation.

A careful answer therefore uses four separate records:

| Compounded preparation | A named prescription-specific preparation, compounder, setting, formulation, presentation, and dated batch records, if supplied. [S2] |
| Transaction | The dispensing source, price, coverage decision, shipping, inventory, and date-specific fulfillment record, if supplied. |

## What Zepbound’s product record establishes

The FDA label names ZEPBOUND as tirzepatide injection for subcutaneous use. [S4] Lilly’s prescribing information uses the same product identity and describes Zepbound as an injection. [S5] These sources are product-specific: they describe the labeled product supplied under the Zepbound name, not every preparation that uses the active-ingredient name tirzepatide.

The label excerpt identifies Zepbound as a GIP and GLP-1 receptor agonist and states that it is indicated with a reduced-calorie diet and increased physical activity for labeled adult populations. It also includes an indication for moderate to severe obstructive sleep apnea in adults with obesity. [S4][S5] Those are label statements, not a conclusion that any reader meets an indication or should use a particular medicine.

The label also lists presentations in strengths of 2.5 mg, 5 mg, 7.5 mg, 10 mg, 12.5 mg, and 15 mg per 0.5 mL in a single-dose pen or single-dose vial. [S4] A presentation list is an identity and labeling fact. It is not a personal dose plan, a recommendation to change products, or a comparison of an unapproved preparation.

The Drugs@FDA record gives a second layer of identity evidence. It lists the active ingredient, dosage form and route, prescription marketing status. It also shows an ORIG-1 approval action dated November 8, 2023. [S6] An application record and a label answer different questions: the application record helps establish the regulatory record, while the label supplies the product-specific prescribing information.

## What “compounded tirzepatide” means here

“Compounded tirzepatide” is not one FDA-approved brand or one universal finished product. In a general discussion, it can describe a preparation made under a compounding pathway. The evidence supplied for this guide does not identify a specific patient prescription, compounder, facility, formulation, concentration, container, lot, sterility result, potency result, or certificate of analysis.

FDA’s compounding Q&A explains that compounding can serve an important patient need when a patient cannot be treated with an FDA-approved medication, such as needing a different dosage form or avoiding an ingredient that causes an allergy. [S2] That explanation describes a possible role for compounding; it does not decide whether a particular preparation is necessary or appropriate for a particular person.

FDA also says compounded drugs should only be used in patients whose medical needs cannot be met by an FDA-approved drug. [S2] This is a source-bound regulatory statement. It is not permission to assume that a named website, pharmacy, or prescription has satisfied every applicable requirement.

The FDA Q&A distinguishes outsourcing facilities under section 503B from pharmacies and physicians operating under section 503A conditions. Drugs compounded in outsourcing facilities are subject to current good manufacturing practice requirements, while the cited section 503A category is not subject to those same CGMP requirements. [S2] This distinction is not a universal quality ranking. It means the legal and oversight setting matters and must be identified before a claim is evaluated.

A source that gives only the ingredient name leaves several questions unanswered. Who prescribed it? Who compounded it? Under which pathway? What exact formulation and presentation were supplied? Which records support the claim? What date applies? What happened at dispensing? If those records are absent, the correct value is `Not stated`, not a favorable assumption.

## FDA approval is not an ingredient synonym

FDA’s current unapproved-GLP-1 statement says compounded drugs are not FDA approved and that the agency does not review them for safety, effectiveness, or quality before they are marketed. [S1] Among the warning signs FDA lists is a company claiming that a compounded drug is the same as an FDA-approved drug. [S1]

That warning matters because “same active ingredient” is a narrower proposition than “same product.” The ingredient proposition can be true while the product-equivalence proposition remains unestablished. A finished medicine can also differ in inactive ingredients, container, device, concentration, labeling, manufacturing setting, controls, and evidence package. The supplied source pack does not validate those fields for an unnamed preparation.

The correct comparison language is therefore conditional and specific. Say that Zepbound is an FDA-approved product containing tirzepatide when the label and application record are the sources. Say that FDA describes compounded drugs as not FDA-approved when the general compounding sources are the sources. Do not turn those two sentences into “the compounded product is Zepbound” or “the two products are interchangeable.”

| “The products share an ingredient name.” | Can be stated only for the specific records that name the ingredient; it does not prove finished-product equivalence. [S4] |

## Label-documented studies and adverse reactions

FDA’s Zepbound label has a boxed warning: tirzepatide caused thyroid C-cell tumors in rats; whether Zepbound causes these tumors, including medullary thyroid carcinoma (MTC), in humans is unknown. Zepbound is contraindicated in patients with a personal or family history of MTC, or in patients with Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia syndrome type 2 (MEN 2). [S4]

This is not the complete safety label.

The FDA label states that the most common adverse reactions reported in at least 5% of patients treated with Zepbound are nausea, diarrhea, vomiting, constipation, abdominal pain, dyspepsia, injection site reactions, fatigue, hypersensitivity reactions, eructation, hair loss, and gastroesophageal reflux disease (GERD). [S4]

For weight reduction, Studies 1 and 2 were randomized, double-blind, placebo-controlled fixed-dosage trials in adults. Studies 5 and 6 included 467 adults with moderate to severe OSA and obesity. [S4]

These Zepbound records do not establish comparable safety, effectiveness, or outcomes for a compounded preparation; FDA says compounded drugs lack premarket safety or effectiveness review. [S1][S4]

## FDA warnings on unapproved and illegally marketed products

FDA reports dosing errors with compounded injectable semaglutide, including some reports requiring hospitalization. Separately, FDA reports adverse events that may be related to compounded semaglutide or tirzepatide prescribed beyond FDA-approved label doses. These are distinct reports, not established tirzepatide-specific dosing-error or hospitalization rates. [S1]

FDA warns that illegally marketed semaglutide and tirzepatide may be counterfeit, contain wrong or harmful ingredients, or contain too little, too much, or no active ingredient. This warning concerns illegally marketed products; it does not identify every compounded drug as counterfeit. [S1]
